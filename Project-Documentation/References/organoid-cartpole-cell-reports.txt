Summary
Experimental neuroscience techniques are advancing rapidly, with developments in high-density electrophysiology and targeted electrical stimulation enabling single-cell-resolution recording and stimulation. Cortical organoids derived from pluripotent stem cells show great promise as
in vitro
models of brain development, function, and disease. In this work, we demonstrate goal-directed learning in brain organoids through feedback-driven neural plasticity. We developed a closed-loop electrophysiology framework to embody mouse cortical organoids into a pole-balancing task (“cartpole”) and evaluated performance improvements when delivering high-frequency training signals. We found that, for most organoids, training signals chosen by artificial reinforcement learning yield better performance than randomly chosen training signals or no training signal, yet improvements do not persist after the 45-min rest period. We further show that training-induced plasticity requires intact glutamatergic transmission, as pharmacological blockade of AMPA and NMDA receptors abolished performance improvements. This systematic approach to studying goal-directed neural plasticity mechanisms
in vitro
opens new possibilities for neural rehabilitation and biological computation.

Results
Multi-phase experimentation
We developed a framework consisting of three key experimental phases to achieve goal-directed learning in cortical organoids: (1) network characterization through spontaneous recording, (2) stimulus-response mapping through targeted electrical stimulation, and (3) closed-loop training in the dynamical task (
Figure 1
A). Each phase builds upon automated analysis from the previous phase to systematically identify and interface with relevant neural circuits. This approach enables us to first characterize the network’s causal connectivity before attempting to dynamically modify the stimulus-evoked responses through training. The framework provides millisecond-precision control to minimize latency between the neural culture and the virtual environment, and it also supports reproducibility through an automated analysis pipeline.

Neural configuration
Our targeted approach to cortical organoid computation focuses on characterizing capabilities within small subcircuits. To do this, we require a few separate components. First, we begin by capturing spontaneous neural activity in the record phase, using it to identify locations of putative neurons and the best electrodes to stimulate them. As shown in a previous study, electrically stimulating the axon initial segment yields the best chance at triggering action potentials, with electrode locations showing higher recorded amplitudes, providing better stimulation efficacy.
52
The spontaneous activity recording is used to generate a spatial map of putative neural unit locations (
STAR Methods
under “spontaneous recording and neural unit identification”) through a metric incorporating firing rate and action potential amplitude (
Figures 2
E and 2F). We then perform signal averaging triggered by the peak amplitude of detected action potentials at these locations to extract spatiotemporal footprints and remove duplicates for each unit. Larger amplitudes yield neurons that are easier to identify in real-time experiments. This analysis enables us to reliably detect neural activity on distinct electrodes throughout the experiment and identify optimal stimulation sites based on peak amplitude locations.
To map causal information flow through these neural circuits, we characterized stimulus-response relationships during the stimulate phase by delivering bi-phasic pulses to each identified neural unit one by one for 50 repetitions (delivered at 2 Hz) as seen in
Figures 3
A and 3C. We used automated analysis to quantify three major temporal response signatures: first-order causal connectivity representing direct neural pathways (
Figure 3
B), multi-order causal connectivity showing network-mediated responses (
Figure 3
D), and probability of evoking network-wide bursts (
Figure S2
). Based on these metrics, we created a neural configuration defining units for encoding (input), decoding (output), and training. We selected two encoding and two decoding units, prioritizing first-order connectivity from encoding to decoding units, and used multi-order connectivity as a secondary preference (
Figures 3
E and 3F). Units exhibiting frequent network bursting responses were deemed less suitable as encoding units since widespread activation could interfere with more fine-grained control. After encoding and decoding units were selected, between 5 and 12 training units were selected, independent of connectivity patterns, to explore optimal training stimulations.

Training paradigm
Using our characterized neural configuration, in the training phase, we trained organoids to balance a pole on the cartpole task, where an inverted pendulum attached to a cart must be balanced by applying horizontal forces (between −10 N [newtons] and 10 N) to the cart (
Figure 1
,
Video S5
). Each episode represents a single attempt at balancing the pole until failure (defined as the pole exceeding a terminal angle of ±16°, which represents a nearly unrecoverable state) and typically lasts for tens of seconds depending on performance. The electrophysiological interaction between the environment simulation and the organoid was specified by a rate-coding scheme
7
,
53
,
54
for both encoding pole angle (stimulating the input units) and decoding horizontal forces (reading smoothed spike counts of the output units) to apply to the cart. This scheme was employed for each discrete time step within an episode (
STAR Methods
under “rate-coding scheme for encoding and decoding”). Each episode ran from a random initialization until failure. A series of consecutive episodes is called a training cycle. We evaluated the performance within a training cycle as the amount of time balanced per episode. A result indicating learning within a training cycle showed an increase in the duration of episodes. To allow for both learning and recovery, experiments were organized typically into 15-min training cycles with 45-min rest periods. The organoid received real-time sensory information about pole angle and provided motor commands throughout each episode. However, training signals—the high-frequency stimulation patterns that modify neural dynamics—were delivered only at episode completion and only when a performance criterion was met. This design ensures that training signals cannot directly influence task performance through immediate artifacts or interference but instead must modify the organoid’s neural dynamics and information processing during subsequent episodes. Specifically, training signals were administered whenever the 5-episode mean performance did not exceed the 20-episode mean, allowing the system to focus on larger-scale adaptations while filtering out the impact of individual episodes of poor performance. If the time balanced never surpassed 10 s within the first training cycle, different encoding/decoding units were selected. See the
STAR Methods
under “cartpole task environment” for further details on training and
Figure S7
for configuration layouts.

Our approach leverages a fundamental property of neural networks: high-frequency stimulation can induce shifts in network dynamics that persist beyond the stimulation period.
9
,
37
By delivering specific paired-pulse patterns to designated training neurons at episode completion, we aim to perturb the network state in ways that modify the input-output transformation between encoding and decoding units. The adaptive selection of these perturbations—choosing which neural pairs to co-activate based on recent performance—allows us to explore different network configurations, effectively searching for control policies that improve task performance. Each stimulation pattern acts as a targeted perturbation that can shift the network’s dynamical landscape, and by tracking which perturbations lead to better balancing, we guide the system toward more effective configurations. Therefore, we call these network perturbations training patterns, and we optimize the training pattern selection using artificial reinforcement-learning principles.
Superior performance of adaptive training signals over random and null conditions
To investigate whether training signals sent to the training neurons could modify information processing between input and output neurons, we compared three distinct experimental conditions: (1) “null,” without stimulation; (2) “random,” with stimulation of five randomly chosen training units (neurons); and (3) “adaptive,” with stimulation of a pair of training units (neurons) selected through reinforcement learning with eligibility traces—a computational method that tracks which neuron pairs have previously led to performance improvements and preferentially selects those pairs for future training while gradually forgetting older associations (
Figures 4
A and 4B). We refer to either a quintuple or a pair of units selected for training stimulation feedback as a “training pattern.” We chose to stimulate randomly ordered quintuples to replicate similar conditions from previous studies.

These results demonstrate both the effectiveness of adaptive training and the importance of proper neural configuration selection. While high-frequency stimulation generally enhanced network performance over baseline, adaptive selection of training signals led to significantly better outcomes than random selection, with proficiency rates doubling in continuous experiments. The state-dependent nature of each cycle’s 90th percentile performance suggests persistent network states conducive to adaptation rather than independent and isolated initializations.

Discussion
Our results demonstrate goal-directed learning in brain organoids using intentionally selected circuits, establishing a new paradigm for biological computation beyond the successful unsupervised reservoir learning approach demonstrated by Cai et al.
22
Organoids naturally recapitulate neural development and, to some extent, circuit architecture in three-dimensional space, providing a highly parallel substrate for spatial computation. HD-MEAs enable millisecond-precision closed-loop control, allowing us to map specific computational roles to individual neurons despite capturing only a subset of the total network activity. Using a novel multi-phase experimental approach, we designed a rapid analysis pipeline that enables iterative experimentation, characterizing neural responses and adjusting parameters within minutes between experimental phases. This capability for systematic investigation mirrors the rapid prototyping that accelerated artificial neural network development. Mouse cortical organoids, with their quick development cycle,
58
,
59
provide an ideal test bed for this engineering approach to biological computation. This framework opens new possibilities for both fundamental neuroscience and hybrid bioelectronic systems.
Unlike previous
in vitro
learning studies focused on pattern recognition or fixed behaviors,
20
,
22
our framework tackles a continuously unstable control problem requiring active state maintenance, demonstrating that biological circuits can optimize complex dynamical systems even without canonical reward circuitry. Our experimental design—delivering training patterns only at episode completion and selecting training units distinct from input/output units—isolates the learning effects. Random stimulation did not significantly improve performance over baseline. However, our adaptive training paradigm, which optimized training signal selection based on performance changes, significantly outperformed both random and null conditions, achieving proficiency in 46.5% of continuous cycles. This demonstrates that biological neural networks can be systematically modified through precise electronic control.
Our adaptive approach represents a hybrid form of reinforcement learning that bridges artificial and biological systems. We use artificial reinforcement learning algorithms with eligibility traces
60
to optimize the selection of training signals delivered to biological neural networks. In this framework, the reinforcement learning algorithm learns which pairs of stimulation sites, when activated together, often improve task performance over time. Unlike canonical biological reinforcement learning, which relies on neuromodulatory signals such as dopamine,
61
our system delivers direct electrical stimulation to selected neurons based solely on task performance metrics. The organoid’s neural dynamics adapt in response to these externally imposed training patterns, improving its input-output mappings for pole balancing. This adaptation likely occurs through stimulation-induced shifts in network dynamics where each training pattern effectively explores a different configuration of the network’s dynamical landscape, with successful patterns reinforcing beneficial network states.
9
,
20
Interestingly, extensive work has shown that cortical neurons, particularly in the prefrontal and orbitofrontal cortex, can represent reward expectation and value signals,
62
,
63
suggesting that cortical circuits may possess intrinsic mechanisms for performance-based optimization. Our results demonstrate that such circuits can be shaped toward specific behavioral goals through systematic electrical stimulation, potentially mimicking endogenous processes by which cortical networks identify and strengthen task-relevant connections.
13
The effectiveness of training signals showed clear state dependence, building on observations from Bakkum et al.
20
that different stimulation patterns succeed at different times. This suggests an interaction between training signals and recent activity history, providing an opportunity to explore fundamental stimulus-based learning rules at the circuit level. We observed rapid modulation of control policies through these brief training cycles while also discovering temporal correlation in the network’s ability to achieve high performance over multi-hour periods, revealing distinct timescales of network modulation. Such adaptation across multiple timescales demonstrates a key advantage of biological systems for hybrid bioelectronic applications: while our training signals enabled rapid tuning of task-specific responses, the observed long-term state dependencies suggest potential for shaping underlying network dynamics to support and enhance these rapid adaptations. Training adaptations in different organoids led to similar control strategies that effectively model inverted pendulum dynamics, even though the details of the neural connectivity in each organoid differ greatly. This convergence toward functionally similar input-output mappings demonstrates the organoids’ ability to develop equivalent control policies through different underlying neural pathways.
Causal connectivity analysis reveals how interfacing with the network influences learning capability, providing a framework for predicting and optimizing performance. The strong predictive power of first-order causal connectivity (
R
2
= 0.59 in proficient cycles) compared with traditional functional connectivity metrics demonstrates the importance of direct, stimulus-evoked pathways in architecting successful learning. The correlation between output units’ ability to evoke network-wide responses and performance suggests that effective neural interfaces benefit from neurons capable of recruiting broader network activity. This methodology of causal characterization could inform both therapeutic interventions and the design of future biological computing systems. By isolating fundamental stimulus-based functionality in simplified circuits, we can systematically identify and explore learning rules that may scale to complex bioelectronic systems.
To test the biological basis of learning, we blocked glutamatergic transmission with the AMPA/kainate receptor antagonist NBQX and the NMDA receptor antagonist APV. Application of these antagonists reduced performance by 64%, which recovered after washout, confirming that learning depends on intact glutamatergic transmission. This establishes a synaptic basis for the observed plasticity. Adaptive training modifies biologically mediated neural dynamics rather than exploiting artifacts of the interface or task structure. Both AMPA and NMDA receptor-mediated neurotransmission is necessary for the adaptive responses. The rapid impairment and recovery suggest that our training paradigm induces changes in neural dynamics that require active glutamatergic signaling, though the precise cellular mechanisms underlying these stimulus-evoked changes remain to be characterized.
Our open-source Python-based platform
BrainDance
(
https://braingeneers.github.io/braindance
) enables exploration of biological learning mechanisms through flexible and accessible experimental design following the rapid iteration paradigm that accelerated machine learning development. By isolating fundamental stimulus-based functionality in simplified circuits and providing tools for systematic investigation, this framework enables identification of learning rules that may scale from organoid models to therapeutic neural interfaces and hybrid bioelectronic computation systems.
Limitations of the study
Our experimental approach has several technical constraints that affect interpretation and generalizability. Planar HD-MEA recordings capture only activity from the organoid surface in contact with the array, missing the three-dimensional network structure above. Single-electrode thresholding for spike detection likely includes multi-unit activity, which hinders understanding connectivity on a per-neuron level; improved neural role assignment and real-time sorting methods
57
could address this limitation.
The observed performance improvements did not persist through the 45-min rest periods between training cycles, requiring relearning in subsequent cycles. This suggests our training paradigm induces short-term plasticity operating on timescales distinct from canonical long-term potentiation. Whether this reflects fundamental constraints of the approach or could be overcome through modified training protocols remains unclear.
Learning capability proved configuration dependent. Even within the same organoid, different selections of input/output neurons yielded varying success rates. Only 22% of adaptive training cycles (45% for continuous adaptive) achieved proficiency, indicating that successful learning under this paradigm requires specific network architecture features we do not yet fully understand. Our manual selection of neural configurations based on causal connectivity, while informed by data, lacks systematic optimization and likely misses better alternatives.
The rate-coding scheme and reinforcement learning parameters (eligibility trace decay and learning rate) were hand-tuned rather than systematically optimized. More sophisticated approaches—adaptive encoding schemes, latent space representations of organoid state, or advanced RL algorithms that incorporate task structure—could improve performance. Integration of local field potentials might provide richer and more stable network state information beyond spike counts.
Future investigations should examine how learning capability changes across organoid developmental stages and with different regional specifications, test systematic methods for neural configuration selection, explore how stimulation timing and pattern structure affect plasticity induction, and identify the cellular mechanisms underlying the glutamatergic-dependent adaptation we observe.

Neural configuration selection
Neural configurations were chosen following a three-part systematic approach. First, we identified encoding units with selective connectivity patterns, characterized by columns in the first-order connectivity matrix where only a small fraction of units showed high response probabilities. We prioritized encoding unit pairs (e.g., A → B and C → D) that maintained independence, avoiding configurations where stimulation of one encoding unit would strongly activate the other encoding unit or the opposite decoding unit (i.e., avoiding strong A → C or A → D connections). When suitable first-order connections were sparse, we used the multi-order connectivity matrix as a secondary criterion. Finally, we excluded units that consistently evoked network-wide bursts (burst probability >0.5), as widespread activation interfered with precise information transmission on the task timescale. Configurations were tested for one training cycle, and if the 90th percentile time balanced did not exceed 10 s, an alternative configuration was selected.
We did not enforce a minimum distance between stimulation electrodes. To avoid redundant detection of the same neuron from multiple electrodes, we employed a conservative footprint deduplication method during the Record phase. Putative spatio-temporal footprints were extracted for each active channel, and pairs sharing more than 50% of their constituent electrodes were identified as redundant. For each redundant pair, we retained only the footprint with the larger peak-to-peak signal amplitude in its spike-triggered average. Cartpole task environment
Cartpole (also known as inverted pendulum) is a traditional control problem which tests an agent’s ability to balance an unstable dynamical system. The task is typically restricted to 2D, involving a cart that can move horizontally balancing a pole vertically that is free to rotate around the attachment joint. Training signal paradigms
Training signals were delivered in a manner to induce a shift in how the network processed inputs. The training patterns were delivered only in response to two conditions being met: 1) termination of the task (pole drops below a terminal angle), and 2) the performance of the 5-episode mean is below the performance of the 20-episode mean. This second criterion results in the system focusing on larger-scale adaptations while ignoring singular episodes with poor performance. When these both are met, we enter the training period, where this is the only stimulation being delivered. Three experimental conditions were tested: a null case without stimulation, and two stimulation paradigms, random and adaptive, with signals administered to the corresponding training neurons. The pool from which individual training neurons and corresponding electrodes (“training electrodes”) were selected to receive feedback stimulation (
n
=8–15) was created in the Record phase based on the activity metric described in Methods: Spontaneous recording and neural unit identification.
The random paradigm delivered sequential biphasic pulses (5 ms inter-pulse interval) through 5 training electrodes selected randomly without replacement from the pool of possible training electrodes. Each stimulation epoch consisted of one complete 5-pulse sequence delivered at 10 Hz for 200 ms.
In contrast to the random paradigm, the adaptive paradigm utilized a weighted sampling approach for paired-pulse patterns (5 ms inter-pulse interval). We construct a pool of all possible training patterns (e.g., if electrodes A, B, C, D, E are available, patterns include (A,B), (A,C), (A,D), …, (D,E)), and sample based on a its historical contribution to performance improvements.
